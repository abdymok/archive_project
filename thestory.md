# A record of days

There are several ways to keep a record of the world. One can begin with presidents, armies, treaties and the solemn announcements of men who have mistaken history for their own correspondence. Or one can begin with a morning, a cup of coffee, an overheard conversation, an unexpected noise in the street. The second method is less imposing, but it has one considerable advantage: the world is obliged to make an appearance.

This archive began before the great rupture. Its earlier entries belong to a world that was already showing signs of illness, although, as is often the case with illness, the patient had other things to do. There were politicians, journalists, oligarchs, assorted visionaries and professional custodians of the obvious. There was the familiar theatre of Ukrainian politics, the peculiar gravity of Moscow, the Western talent for misunderstanding events while explaining them at length. There were jokes, irritations, excursions, arguments and the small private business of being alive.

Then came February 24, 2022.

Day 1.

A date can be a calendar mark, but it can also become a border. On one side is the life we thought we were leading. On the other is the life that has been assigned to us. The distinction is not always immediately apparent. People still have suitcases to pack, errands to run, messages to answer. The sun rises with its customary indifference. Somewhere, somebody is looking for a charger. Somewhere else, somebody is trying to decide whether to leave Kyiv.

The war did not abolish ordinary life. It entered it, took up residence and began rearranging the furniture.

From that day onward, the entries became a record of a country at war and of one observer’s attempts to make sense of what was happening around him. Not a military history, not an official chronicle, not a claim to comprehensiveness. The archive has no pretence of being a complete account of Ukraine, and certainly no ambition to speak for everyone who lives here. It is a situated record, written from Kyiv, with all the advantages and limitations of proximity. Sometimes the view is broad. Sometimes it extends only as far as the next street, the nearest metro station or the window from which an explosion can be heard.

There are days when events seem to explain themselves. More often they require explanation, and sometimes they resist it. The absurdity of public life continues even under bombardment. Indeed, it can become more conspicuous. The solemn and the ridiculous travel together, occasionally in the same vehicle. A joke may be an evasion, but it may also be a form of accuracy. We have tried to preserve both the gravity of events and the comedy that refuses to leave them alone.

What follows is therefore not simply a collection of posts arranged by date. It is a record of attention: to people, places, language, power, destruction, resilience and the strange persistence of daily life. The entries accumulate. Their meaning changes as the days pass. A remark made in one year may acquire an unexpected weight in another.

Day 1 is the turning point. It is not the beginning of the story. It is the day the story changed its terms.

## Prelude to war

In the weeks before February 24, 2022, the future was behaving rather badly. It kept sending messages, making telephone calls and appearing in public places, but no one seemed quite certain what to make of it. Some said an invasion was imminent. Others insisted it was not inevitable. Still others explained that the difference between these two words was important, although the distinction seemed to escape a number of people whose professional business was explaining things.

The Russians, meanwhile, were assembling an army on Ukraine’s borders. They were also assembling arguments. Moscow’s preferred version of reality was a curious construction in which Russia had not occupied Crimea, had not started the war in Donbas, had no intention of invading Ukraine and was, in fact, the injured party. It was a remarkable achievement of political architecture. One could almost admire the workmanship, were it not for the rather large number of tanks parked outside.

In Kyiv, the atmosphere was more complicated. American officials were warning of an approaching catastrophe, while Ukrainian officials frequently appeared more concerned about the effects of the warnings than the possibility of the catastrophe itself. There were arguments about blood supplies, intelligence, evacuation plans, airspace, insurance and the proper use of English adverbs. The President was reassuring foreign journalists. His advisers were congratulating him on his messaging. The defence minister was telling the country that the likelihood of invasion was low. Somewhere between Washington’s alarms and Kyiv’s reassurances, ordinary people were expected to make up their own minds.

The Western response offered its own spectacle. European leaders travelled to Moscow to visit the Dragon, as though a sufficiently elaborate display of diplomatic courtesy might persuade him to become a house pet. Macron went. Scholz was expected. Everyone had a proposal, a formula or a meeting in principle. The Americans, meanwhile, were warning that the Dragon was not merely hungry but had brought a rather large appetite. Ukraine found itself caught between those who wanted to negotiate away the danger and those who seemed prepared to describe it in such alarming terms that the country itself became part of the damage.

There was also the familiar procession of experts, commentators, journalists and professional Ukraine enthusiasts. Some were useful. Others had discovered that proximity to a crisis was an excellent way to acquire an audience, even if one had little to say. The distinction between reporting and performance became increasingly difficult to maintain. We watched people manufacture certainty from fragments of intelligence, repeat rumours as analysis and congratulate themselves for having understood a country they had barely bothered to observe.

And yet, beneath the noise, something serious was taking shape. The Kremlin’s demands were becoming less obscure. Its language was becoming more openly imperial. The possibility that Russia might attempt to destroy Ukrainian independence was no longer a theoretical exercise conducted by people with maps and television studios. The question was how far Moscow intended to go, and how much of the world was prepared to let it.

These entries were written without knowledge of what would happen next. They contain impatience, sarcasm, arguments, occasional excursions into chicken recipes and the usual irritation with people who mistake confidence for competence. They also contain a growing recognition that the situation was becoming dangerous in ways that no diplomatic euphemism could conceal.

On February 23, the story was still being described as a tragicomedy. The next morning, the comedy would become considerably harder to sustain.

Day 1 was approaching. The prelude was almost over.

## The first thirty days

On February 24, 2022, the argument ended. Russia invaded Ukraine, and the future that had been discussed, predicted, denied and occasionally mocked arrived in the form of missiles, explosions and columns of military vehicles.

The first entries are full of movement and uncertainty. *Wandering around with suitcases*. *Remember Det-A?* *Enemy sabotage groups*. Reports came from Hostomel, Obolon, Vasylkiv and the streets of Kyiv. There were sirens, gunfire, roadblocks and rumours of infiltrators. Residential buildings were struck. Bridges were blown up. The capital was preparing to defend itself.

The city nevertheless continued to function, after a fashion. Curfew ended. People went to work. A mother pushed a baby carriage loaded with water containers through an almost empty centre. The weather was cold and bright. There were children in camouflage and civilians carrying weapons. The ordinary world had not disappeared. It had acquired an unfamiliar set of instructions.

The first week brought its own vocabulary: *Demonstrative acts of extreme violence*. *Giant pink blob*. *Stay out of doorways!* The enormous Russian convoy approaching Kyiv became the principal object of military speculation. Maps multiplied, experts debated and television correspondents explained. The convoy stalled. The invasion’s promised lightning victory was becoming something else.

The early confidence of the Russian leadership rested on a remarkable misunderstanding of Ukraine. The country was expected to submit, its government to disappear and its population to welcome a new administration. Even reports from inside the Russian security apparatus suggested that the plan had come apart. Denazification and demilitarisation were proving difficult to translate into measurable achievements.

Meanwhile, the geography of the war became painfully familiar. Borodyanka, Irpin and Bucha were no longer names on maps. Russian troops moved through towns and villages around Kyiv, occupying buildings, terrorising residents and killing civilians. People attempting to escape were shot. The destruction of Borodyanka was particularly difficult to comprehend. A place that had once been a familiar destination for a bicycle ride had become a landscape of ruins.

Farther away, Mariupol was being subjected to a siege of extraordinary cruelty. *Mariupol massacre*. The bombing of a maternity hospital. Hundreds of thousands of people trapped, without reliable access to food, water or medical care. The war was no longer merely an attempt to capture territory. It was an assault on the people who lived there.

And still, the archive retained its peculiar sense of proportion. A shoe was repaired because the repairmen were busy fighting the invaders. A turtle named Torpedo was rescued after a missile strike in Obolon. A cruise missile was intercepted over Kyiv. The wreckage fell in Kurenivka. *Torpedo the flying turtle* became, briefly, an entry in the history of military affairs.

The absurdities did not make the violence less serious. They made the world recognisable.

By the third week, the maps showed Russian forces digging in around Kyiv rather than advancing towards it. The approaches to the capital, the roads and settlements west, north and east of the city, became increasingly important. *More smart optics, please*. The war had acquired a geography of its own, and understanding it required more than coloured shapes and confident television commentary.

The international conversation was becoming familiar, too. Sanctions, negotiations, red lines and assurances that Western governments were not parties to the conflict. Ukraine was receiving weapons and intelligence, but it needed more. Advice was plentiful. Ammunition was more useful.

The archive was also recording the curious population that gathers around every catastrophe: serious volunteers, military specialists, journalists, commentators, adventurers and people who seem to regard danger as a promising career opportunity. Some were doing indispensable work. Others were performing for an audience. *Mad Max*. *TikTok legionnaires*. Even a war has its travelling circus.

On March 25, the thirtieth day, Kyiv was still standing. The original Russian plan had failed. The government remained in place, the armed forces continued to resist, and the capital had not been captured.

But the month did not end in triumph. Mariupol remained under siege. Towns around Kyiv lay devastated. Millions of people had been displaced. The dead could not be restored by the failure of the invasion’s opening campaign.

Thirty days had altered the meaning of ordinary things. A journey of forty kilometres could become a military operation. A walk to work could be an act of endurance. A repaired shoe, a rescued turtle and a quiet morning could become matters worth recording.

The archive had begun as a collection of observations. It was now something else: a record of a country refusing to disappear.

There were still 1,709 days to go before the count reached 1,739. There was no way of knowing what they would contain.

## The next three months

The first month ended with Kyiv still standing and the Russian army looking for another way to win a war it had expected to finish in a few days. The next three months would be devoted to discovering what that other way might look like.

The geography changed. The enormous convoy that had threatened the capital became a memory. The fighting moved east and south, towards Donbas, where the names of towns began to accumulate with the grim regularity of entries in a ledger. The first phase was over, though the archive would not formally announce it until April 21, under the rather belated heading *Phase 1 ended on March 25*.

There was a brief temptation to believe that the worst had passed. Kyiv began to breathe again. People returned. Cars appeared on familiar roads. The city grew greener, cafés prepared to reopen, and the monuments received the attention usually reserved for visiting dignitaries. But the places left behind by the Russian army were telling a different story. *One big crime scene* was not a metaphor. Bucha, Irpin and Borodyanka had become evidence. The dead were no longer distant figures in daily reports. Their bodies, and the houses around them, recorded what the occupation had meant.

The war continued, and the arguments about it multiplied. There were maps, phases, forecasts, weapons lists and elaborate theories about what Putin might do next. There were also the familiar experts, some of whom had been wrong before the invasion and were now being invited to explain its progress. *Outrageous claims to expertise* became a recurring subject. The archive was learning that a war could be fought on several fronts at once, and that not all of them were military.

In May, the Russian offensive in the east became the central concern. *The Popasna salient*. *The Sievierodonetsk salient*. *The Lyman salient*. The names were less familiar than Kyiv’s suburbs, but the arithmetic was becoming unmistakable. Russia was concentrating its forces, destroying towns and trying to grind its way forward. Ukraine needed weapons, ammunition and equipment. The requests were repeated so often that they began to sound less like diplomacy than a household reminder left on the kitchen table.

There was a curious interval between the announcement of assistance and its arrival. Politicians made statements. Officials discussed deliveries. Analysts assessed capabilities. Somewhere in the middle, soldiers needed the equipment. *Weapons, weapons, weapons* was not an especially sophisticated strategic doctrine, but it had the advantage of being comprehensible.

Mariupol remained the great wound. Its siege, the destruction of the city and the fate of the defenders at Azovstal stood behind the daily accounts of fighting elsewhere. The Russian parade on May 9 offered its own grotesque theatre. Moscow celebrated victory over fascism while prosecuting a war of destruction against a neighbouring country. History, as usual, had been invited to provide the decorations.

By June, the first hundred days had passed. *100-Day wrap-up* was a convenient label for an inconvenient reality. The invasion had failed to achieve its original objectives, but the war was nowhere near over. Russian forces continued to advance in parts of Donbas. Ukrainian cities remained under attack. The supply of Western weapons was still a matter of argument, negotiation and delay.

There were lighter entries, too. A vegetable preference involving pelmeni. A farm visited by Mario, Emmanuel and Olaf. The occasional encounter with a visitor, a journalist or an expert whose understanding of Ukraine seemed inversely proportional to the confidence with which it was expressed. Such things belonged in the record. They were not distractions from the war so much as evidence that life had not agreed to become exclusively about it.

The chapter closes in late June with a simple instruction: *Eat, sleep, fight*. By then the war had acquired a rhythm, though not a predictable one. The first shock had given way to endurance. The capital was no longer in immediate danger of occupation, but the country was paying for that reprieve in the east.

Three months had passed. The invasion had not ended. It had changed shape, and so had the archive.

## The long middle

By the summer of 2022, the war had settled into a new and less comprehensible shape. Kyiv was no longer the immediate prize. The Russian army had been driven from the capital’s approaches, but the country remained under attack along a front stretching from the northeast to the Black Sea. The first shock had passed. The war was becoming a test of endurance, equipment, organisation and patience. Patience, as it turned out, was in particularly short supply.

The summer brought a great deal of waiting. Weapons were arriving, but never quite quickly enough. The front moved slowly. Russian forces dug in, Ukrainian forces prepared, and the daily maps offered more coloured shapes than useful answers. *More stalemate and energetic dancing* was the diagnosis in August. The prospect of a counteroffensive in Kherson was discussed with growing anticipation, though the people doing the actual fighting were rather less inclined to advertise their plans.

Behind the front lines, occupation had its own geography. Kherson was not simply a city waiting to be liberated. It was a region of abductions, intimidation, filtration and disappearance. The accounts were difficult to reconcile with the tidy language of military analysis. A map could show who controlled a road. It could not show what happened in a house after dark.

Then came September, and the pivot. Ukrainian forces broke through Russian positions in the Kharkiv region, recovering territory with a speed that unsettled the usual commentators. The maps changed almost overnight. Russian troops abandoned equipment and positions, and the confident language of Moscow’s military announcements became increasingly difficult to maintain. Kherson remained a harder problem, but the direction of travel was becoming clearer.

Putin responded with mobilisation and the annexation of territory his army did not fully control. The ceremonies in Moscow were conducted with the customary solemnity, as though a document and a stage set could accomplish what the military had failed to do. *Putin’s doom loop* was an apt title. The invasion had produced a sequence of decisions that narrowed his options while making the consequences more dangerous.

October brought the war back to the cities with renewed force. On October 10, Russian missiles struck targets across Ukraine, including Kyiv. The attacks on civilian and energy infrastructure became a campaign of their own. Bridges, power stations, heating systems and the ordinary machinery of urban life were now part of the battlefield. The capital’s glass bridge narrowly escaped destruction. The crater was a reminder that the distance between a morning run and a missile strike could be measured in metres.

The rhetoric in Moscow grew stranger as the military situation deteriorated. Nazism gave way to Satanism. Dirty bombs and combat mosquitoes joined the catalogue of inventions. The explanations changed, but the purpose remained the same: to make the destruction of Ukraine sound like an act of defence.

By November, the war had entered the apartment. Electricity disappeared for hours. Water became uncertain. Elevators stopped, heating systems faltered and the city learned to organise itself around the availability of a socket. The government established points of invincibility, which was an unusually ambitious name for a place where one might charge a telephone and warm one’s hands. Kherson was liberated, but the relief came with the knowledge of what its residents had endured and what the retreating Russians had left behind.

December brought mud, cold, generators and another round of missile attacks. The war was approaching its three-hundredth day. The capital was still functioning, though not always according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The archive kept returning to the same questions: what could be destroyed, what could be defended, and what kind of country would emerge from the wreckage?

The year ended with *The empty chair technique* and a presidential performance that seemed to mistake defiance for infallibility. There were still arguments about weapons, strategy, corruption, foreign assistance and the people appointed to explain it all. The war had not made Ukrainian public life simpler. It had made its contradictions more expensive.

At the start of 2023, Russia had failed to conquer Ukraine. Ukraine had recovered substantial territory, but much remained occupied. The front was still there. So were the missiles, the dead, the displaced and the long winter ahead.

The first thirty days had been a shock. The next three months had established the war’s geography. The remainder of 2022 showed what it would cost to change it.

## The year of the long argument

The year opened with *Splodges of tomato paste*, a small meditation on Twitter, its expanding character limit and the possibility that giving people more room might not make them think any better. It was an appropriate beginning. The war was entering its second year, and there was no shortage of people with room to explain it. The January entry *Pre-experiencing Ukraine's PTSD* turned its attention to the material being piped through the internet, much of it garbage, with the occasional unopened bottle of beer to be found in the dumpster.

The practical questions were less entertaining. Ukraine needed ammunition, night vision, air defence and long-range weapons. Russia had a sanctuary in occupied Crimea from which to attack. NATO officials spoke of strategy while resisting the implications of their own analysis. The archive's January title *NATO wants us dead* was not a diplomatic formulation. Nor was it intended to be. On February 24, *Content-free peace* marked the anniversary. The sun was bright over Kyiv. China had produced a twelve-point plan, a word salad that did not require much digestion. The war had entered its second year, but the argument about what would end it had barely moved.

There were questions closer to home. *The sham democracy* appeared on the eve of the anniversary, reminding readers that the invasion had not abolished Ukraine's political problems. Corruption, appointments, the conduct of the President's Office and the weakness of institutions remained matters of consequence. They did not become less important because Russia was trying to destroy the country. The archive had little interest in the idea that wartime criticism was somehow a luxury.

Spring brought the counteroffensive into public conversation long before it could be brought into operation. The expectations grew faster than the supply of equipment. *Counteroffensive bloat* was the archive's diagnosis of the atmosphere, as journalists and experts competed to predict an operation whose details Ukraine had good reason not to advertise. In April the old Dragon returned. In Evgeny Schwartz's parable, the monster was not destroying the enemy because it had not yet had its fill of war. *The dragons are back* and *Dragon, the parable* were more than literary diversions. The archive saw something familiar in the spectacle of a country waiting for its own deliverance while the people with the power to provide it continued to discuss the terms.

In June, Russia blew up the Kakhovka dam. The flood spread through the lower Dnipro, carrying away homes, roads, animals and the comfortable fiction that civilian infrastructure was somehow outside the war. The archive called it *After the Kakhovka dam*. A few weeks later, Yevgeny Prigozhin sent his Wagner column towards Moscow. The mutiny briefly exposed the peculiar foundations of Russian power. In Kyiv, meanwhile, cobblestones on Bohdan Khmelnytsky Street were being lifted, inspected and put back at considerable public expense. The war and the paving work occupied the same city. They were not competing for space.

July brought the death of Victoria Amelina, who had been documenting Russian war crimes. The archive's entry *The death of Victoria Amelina* appeared amid the continuing arguments about the counteroffensive. At Vilnius, NATO offered Ukraine a future invitation without a timetable. Two months into the operation, *The collective minefield* described the practical obstacle: Ukrainian forces still lacked the equipment to negotiate the minefields Russian troops had spent months laying. There was not much mystery about what was needed. There was a great deal of discussion about whether it would be supplied.

Prigozhin was dead by August. The counteroffensive continued, though the maps became less encouraging. The front moved very little. In November, *The battlefield stalemate* gave the situation its plainest name. Zaluzhny had explained what would be required to move from positional to manoeuvre warfare. The argument was not especially complicated. The means of doing it were another matter.

Meanwhile, the war in Gaza competed for attention, American aid became entangled in domestic politics, and another winter approached. The archive marked *Ten years of war*, a reminder that February 2022 had not created the conflict. It had enlarged it beyond the scale at which the outside world could comfortably pretend not to notice.

December brought a new round of arguments about mobilisation, the President and the Commander-in-Chief. Aid was in doubt, the front remained largely fixed, and the largest air attack of the war struck Ukraine on December 29.

The year ended without a settlement, a breakthrough or a convincing explanation from the people paid to supply them. The archive had begun by proposing to lift the curtain on the nonsense surrounding the war. It had found that the curtain was part of the scenery.

The arguments continued. The war did not wait for them to finish.

Day 674. The next year was already arriving.

## The year of the thinning line

The year began with a digression to Taipei. *The road to Taipei City* compared two electoral democracies of roughly equal size, each threatened by a larger neighbour. It was an unusual way to begin a year in which Ukraine would once again depend on decisions made far away. The last week of December had brought the largest air attack of the war. Congress had gone home, the Speaker was busy with his telephone, and the President of the United States was recovering on a tropical island.

The shortages were becoming harder to disguise. Ukraine needed shells, air defence and men. The third shortage was the most difficult to discuss. The mobilisation argument that had simmered since the autumn came to the boil, while the President and the Commander-in-Chief contradicted each other with increasing regularity. The weeks-long dismissal of Valeriy Zaluzhny was conducted as a tragicomedy, announced, denied, postponed and finally delivered on February 8. Nine days later Avdiivka was abandoned. The foreign press called it the Kremlin's first great victory since Bakhmut. The archive was less impressed by the terminology. The purpose of the battle had been to kill as many invaders as possible, and the invaders had been killed in considerable numbers. Two days later came *Requiem*. There was no attempt to make the loss sound better than it was.

The spring belonged to Washington. For months, Speaker Mike Johnson had conducted his private foreign policy through telephone calls, White House visits and the strategic deployment of evangelical hesitation. *Mike Johnson treads carefully* was one of the archive's more economical descriptions of the performance. In April the aid bill finally passed. Relief was real, but the delay had already acquired consequences. Russian forces broke through at Ocheretyne, and in May a new offensive began near Kharkiv. The invaders advanced against Ukrainian troops who were outnumbered and exhausted. The archive had been warning about the missing ammunition for months. The maps now supplied the illustration.

There were other reasons to be anxious. In March, gunmen attacked a concert hall near Moscow, and the Kremlin hurried to assemble an explanation in which Ukraine had a part. A Tsirkon missile came down over Kyiv. By June, electricity was again being rationed, and the city had relearned the habits of the previous winter, this time with summer heat added to the inconvenience. Day 800 passed in May. In June, the war entered its eleventh year. Neither was a celebration. The count simply continued.

Then, in August, Ukrainian forces crossed into Kursk region. The operation surprised Moscow and, by most accounts, quite a few people in Kyiv. It lifted morale and gave the commentators something new to explain. It did not, however, stop the Russian advance in Donetsk region. The archive put the two developments beside each other under *Pokrovsk packs its bags*. A bold stroke in one place had not changed the arithmetic in another. Toretsk was in trouble, Vuhledar fell, and Kurakhove was next in line. North Korea supplied ballistic missiles, and by December its soldiers had joined the fighting in Kursk region.

In September the President carried his Victory Plan to Washington and discovered that it had become a minor episode in an American election. *The Victory Plan backfires* was the archive's verdict. In November Donald Trump won. The archive called it *Mandate for mayhem*. A quick end to the war was now being promised by a man who would have to persuade the other side to agree. The Economist suggested that the fighting might wind down in 2025. Somebody proposed that Russia keep Crimea. The archive answered with *Peace talks, nuclear annihilation*. It was not in a mood to negotiate away other people's territory.

The winter brought a new missile, Oreshnik, and the old problem of trying to understand what Moscow wanted the world to believe about it. Western governments had finally allowed Ukraine to use long-range missiles against targets in Russia. Putin announced that this meant direct Western participation in the war. The missile reportedly carried no explosives. Its owner was *The maniac next door*. At home, defective mortar shells continued to reach front-line units, with no one taking responsibility for the failure. In December a Russian general in charge of chemical and biological defence was killed by a *Scooter bomb*. Moscow's air defence apparently shot down an Azerbaijani passenger plane.

Through it all there were tulips in April, and *The cherry blossoms survive*. There was stair-running in the dark during the blackouts, a giant goldfish in Iceland and a bicycle brought out of winter storage. Such things were not interruptions. They were the other half of the record.

By December the thousandth day and the beginning of a third winter belonged to the same unfinished sentence. The line had thinned, though it had not broken. Ukraine had lost ground, and learned again how much of its future depended on decisions made elsewhere.

On December 31, ballistic and cruise missiles were over Kyiv. At least one MiG-31 remained airborne when the archive made its last entry of the year, under the title *Quels cons*.

Day 1042. The next year was already arriving.

## The year of the peace process

The year began with a drone that hit a residential flat a stone’s throw from the President’s Office, and with a New Year’s speech delivered, as the archive noted, facing northeast. It was a fitting preface. The war was entering its fourth year, and for the first time in a long while the question was no longer whether the West would help Ukraine fight but whether it would help Ukraine stop. A new American administration took office on January 20, and a quick end to the war, in a day or so, had been among its campaign promises. Kyiv responded with flattery. So did most of Europe. The archive filed the spectacle under *Golden age of brown noses* and went back to counting missiles.

The first months were spent learning a new vocabulary. There were truces and ceasefires, frameworks and non-papers, and a long argument about whether the first two words meant the same thing. The February meeting in the Oval Office did not go as planned. The cameras caught the quarrel, and the minerals agreement remained unsigned. Within days, Washington suspended military assistance. But the rupture did not make Ukraine’s own failures disappear. In January the archive had listed four: fortifications, conscription, new brigades and domestic arms production. It reported that one in ten soldiers was absent without leave. On March 1, an Iskander strike on a training camp in the Dnipropetrovsk region killed and wounded more than a hundred recruits from the hastily assembled 157th Mechanized Brigade. The new commander of the land forces promised that those who had failed to act in time would answer for it. Washington’s envoy, Steve Witkoff, made one trip to Moscow after another, and each one produced the same result. On April 13, Palm Sunday, two ballistic missiles struck the centre of Sumy, killing more than a dozen people, including half a dozen children, a few hours after the American President had said the talks were going fine. The archive recorded it under *Palm Sunday massacre*. The talks, it observed, were not going fine.

By May the peace process had acquired its own rhythm, which was the rhythm Moscow preferred. Delegations met in Istanbul. A thirty-day truce was proposed and politely ignored. CNN observed that the process was going exactly as the Kremlin wanted, which was slowly, and the archive agreed, noting that the war had become more deadly rather than less since the new administration took office. Then, on June 1, Ukraine reminded everyone that it could still act on its own. Drones were concealed in shipping containers disguised as ordinary modular homes and launched against airfields, and the archive’s count had them destroying more than a third of Russia’s strategic bomber fleet. It was one of the few events of the year that did not depend on somebody else’s telephone call. The NATO summit in June disappointed, as summits tend to do.

In August the process reached its theatrical peak. Witkoff went to see Putin, and the archive described the result as *The Neverland summit*: a visitor with pixie dust, a host with a hook and a fleet, and a row of Lost Boys huddled in video calls, waiting for Peter Pan to come back with promises. A week later the two presidents met at a military base in Anchorage. The photographers got their pictures. Two days afterwards Reuters outlined Putin’s terms, which called for withdrawal from Donbas, non-aligned status for Ukraine and the return of the Russian language and the Moscow-linked church, and the archive filed them under *Terms of our surrender*. Kyiv and the Europeans offered their own blueprint, and nothing was agreed. At the end of the month, Andriy Parubiy, the former speaker of the Rada, was shot dead in Lviv, and the police announced that the killing had been carefully prepared.

The autumn belonged to the sky. In September drones crossed into Poland, the alliance recalled that Article 5 existed and issued a strongly worded statement, and the archive, quoting Zaluzhny’s old warning that NATO might end up like the United Nations, called the episode *NATO’s funeral pyre*. In October Putin announced that the delivery of Tomahawk missiles would destroy relations with Washington, even as his armies were bombing Lviv, and the archive wrote *Tomahawks and red lines*. Stalemate, it observed, was attrition’s confession of failure. On October 10, around two in the morning, a drone struck a seventeen-storey building in Pechersk and half the city lost power. The archive had published a piece on turning off the lights two days earlier and did not enjoy being right. It recorded the night under *Kyiv loses power*. By November, an officer was publicly asking for an order to withdraw from Pokrovsk and Myrnohrad before the garrisons were lost, and in December the archive listed what remained of Toretsk, Pokrovsk, Kostiantynivka and Myrnohrad, towns that had been home to hundreds of thousands of people.

Then, in the third week of November, two things arrived at once. Washington produced a peace plan of twenty-eight points, which the archive read as Putin’s maximalist demands from Istanbul in 2022 with an American cover. The same week, anti-corruption investigators disclosed Operation Midas, more than a thousand wiretaps over fifteen months, documenting a kickback scheme at the heart of the energy sector. The archive put the two together under *Surrender diplomacy meets golden toilet mafia*, and the President’s chief of staff, Andriy Yermak, resigned on Friday, November 28. Nobody was quite sure who was running the show in Kyiv. The Americans, for their part, were busy. Witkoff and Jared Kushner went to Moscow, the telephone call that Witkoff apparently placed to someone in Moscow by accident became *Witkoff’s Moscow butt dial*, and by the middle of December the archive was reporting on *The peace plan nobody read*.

December brought a twenty-point plan and the usual assurances. Europe agreed to borrow ninety billion euros to keep Ukraine solvent for two more years, putting aside for now the idea of using frozen Russian assets, which Washington had asked it to do. The Americans logged thirty hours of meetings in a single week, and the President declared that he was tired of meetings for the sake of meetings. Meanwhile the capital was running out of light. *Kyiv goes dark* was the heading on December 12, and DTEK’s outage schedule read like a diary of the city’s evenings.

Through all of this the archive kept its proportions. There were full moons, recorded as faithfully as air raids, and Christmas decorations that were still up at the end of April. There was spring in Kyiv again, and dead hangs and deep squats, and a great many fruitless attempts to explain the weather to foreign correspondents. These entries did not interrupt the war. They proved that people were still living around it.

The last entry of the year was about resilience centres. Reuters had visited Vyshhorod, a small town just north of Kyiv, where people sat out days-long power cuts in crowded shelters. The archive paired the account with a photograph of a rally in Minsk in 2006, and titled the entry *Vyshhorod, Minsk, and resilience*. The diplomats had spent the year drawing up plans for a future. In Vyshhorod, people were trying to get through the present.

The peace process had produced a great many plans and no peace. The war had become the one reliable fixture in a year of promises.

Day 1406. The next year was already arriving.


## The year of two clocks

The year opened with a question, which in Kyiv is the polite way of declining to give an answer. Was it a year to forget, or a year to regret? The New York Times had printed eighteen and a half thousand words on the last day of December about how Washington had helped Moscow prolong the war, and the President, instead of apologising, delivered a pep talk of the kind a man gives a room when he would prefer not to be asked about the room. The archive listened, as it had promised to listen, and went out into the cold.

The cold was the first great subject of 2026. *Welcome to the blockade*, the archive wrote in January, and it did not exaggerate. A city that had once held three and a half million people now held perhaps three million of them, and the missiles arrived on schedule: two hundred and forty-two drones and thirteen ballistic missiles in a single night, with an Oreshnik thrown in for Lviv, as a host might add a final course nobody had ordered. The electricity went off in the morning and came back in the evening, like a tenant of irregular habits. The archive made a study of the cold and concluded, under the long title *How the Cold Was Made: A Kyiv Resident’s Guide to the Arctic Squid Game*, that it had been manufactured partly in Moscow and partly in the district offices, where the insulation of a hundred and nineteen buildings had been contracted at inflated prices and a fifth of the money had gone elsewhere to keep warm. The government celebrated *Nine Megawatts of Victory*, the extra power allotted to a capital of three million people, which at wholesale prices came to a modest 765 euros an hour. Nobody was ungrateful. It was only that gratitude had begun to require arithmetic.

Diplomacy, meanwhile, was having a very good winter. In February the American President announced that things were going well with Ukraine and Russia, and a few days later that the talks had been very, very good and that something could be happening. The archive did not doubt it. It merely wondered where. The fourth anniversary of the invasion passed on February 24, and the archive, which distrusted grief that arrived on a schedule, wrote instead about hollow columns, which look perfectly sound until the moment they are asked to bear a load. Two days later, in Geneva, two men with a full calendar discussed Iran’s nuclear programme in the morning and Ukraine’s survival after lunch, in a hotel on the Rhône, with the same smiles for the same photographers. Both files, it seemed, were negotiable. The archive called the day *The golden age of hegemony*. It was not praise. In March Washington’s attention moved to Iran, and for some weeks Ukraine had the unfamiliar experience of being the second item on the agenda.

The archive’s own contribution to the peace process was a Valentine’s Day story about Napoleon cakes, a young man rumoured to be a sniper who spent his mornings in a fitness studio, and a capitulation with custard on top. It was called *The art of the non-deal*, and it explained, in its way, more than most communiqués.

By April the imagination of the age had begun to work in earnest. The archive recorded a proposal, put forward by people who understood the value of a good overcoat in a hard winter, to name a depopulated strip of coal country in the Dnipropetrovsk region after the most powerful man in the world. They called it *Donnyland*. The archive, which had heard a great deal in four years, noted only that a man who perfects the art of kneeling is liable to forget how to stand, and that it had probably invented the saying that very morning.

In May, anti-corruption investigators released a recording of a conversation dated February 16, 2022, allegedly between the President’s former chief of staff and his house designer. It was eight days before the invasion. The archive, which had spent that same week writing about air drums and roast chicken, observed that it had not been the only one in Kyiv thinking about interiors. It called the entry *The house burns*. The house, in the archive’s usage, was the country. The designer, one gathered, had been working on another one.

June was quieter, by the standards of the year. The archive discovered, and reported under the title *The rebrand that saved Ukraine*, that the Kiev Post had become the Kyiv Post in March 1997, by its calculation largely in order to sell more advertising. It also sat down to write a preamble to itself, covering 2021 to 2026, and gave it a title with a certain modesty: *The map keeps getting larger*. Maps do.

July was not quiet. A strike on Kyiv arrived in its usual order, ballistic missiles first, then jet-propelled drones, then cruise missiles and more drones, and the President warned the city in advance, and the city dutifully carried its foam mats to the metro. The targets were an apartment block in Podil, a hotel, an ambulance station and a monastery with a UNESCO plaque, an inventory of places with no front line to speak of. A few days later a town called Vyshneve, twelve kilometres from the capital, was blown up. The President’s speech reduced the event to a single clause about secondary detonation, and the archive, reaching for Vonnegut, found that the only honest title was *Durable until Tuesday*. So it goes.

Politics produced its own weather. A minister was dismissed, gave an exit briefing that named everyone except the one person who mattered, and left the archive asking *Who is Vova*, a question it would go on asking until the end of the year. It was one year since the President had signed the law that gutted the independence of the anti-corruption agencies and set off the biggest protests of the war, until the protests of that very week. By August the government had been sacked, and the country was without a defence minister or a foreign minister. A flea market in Obolon was struck by its third ballistic missile in two months. At the end of the month an arms depot exploded at Myla, twenty-five kilometres from Kyiv, killing at least thirty-eight people.

In September the spies fought each other. A shootout between two of the country’s security services, with a deputy commander of the Russian Volunteer Corps, who was not even Ukrainian, at its centre, left the President furious and the archive with the heading *Who owns the hostage?* It was less a spy thriller, the archive observed, than a turf war between armed men who had forgotten where the fence stood.

All year the envoys went on shuttling between Paris, Abu Dhabi, Geneva and Miami, like matchmakers at a village wedding, and the Ukrainian saying for the result is that they married him off without him. The archive wrote *Washington hands Moscow the table*, and reread its own dispatches to see how the proposed marriage looked from Kyiv. In October the First Lady’s office announced a local ceasefire at Oleshky, where people foraged for weeds, so that civilians might leave. The archive filed it under *Oleshky for Election Day*, the American midterms being a week away. On November 5 the President, after meeting a Russian envoy, promised a *Big, beautiful agreement*, which would involve more than Ukraine, perhaps all of Europe, and in which everyone would be thrilled. The same week, the leader of the President’s parliamentary faction was received by the First Lady at the White House, and in Kyiv it emerged that senior law-enforcement officials had been working with, or for, a drug cartel with Russian connections based in Mexico.

In Berlin, a spokesman announced that four principles had been agreed in Abu Dhabi, the first being that peace in Ukraine would be discussed first and the wider order for Europe afterwards. Governments rarely announce their principles, the archive remarked. They prefer to let them be discovered later, like an old coat in a wardrobe. On November 19 the leaders of America, Russia and China met at Xi’s invitation and issued a declaration pledging to reform the United Nations, avoid nuclear war and keep international courts from abusing their jurisdiction. The archive, reading point three, understood why. It called the document *Declared impunity*, and the photograph that accompanied it was taken in the Kyiv metro during a combined air assault.

A Washington columnist wrote that the war was running on two clocks, one belonging to the diplomats, which moved slowly, and one belonging to the drones, which moved fast. He reported that Kyiv had spent 172 hours under air alert in October. The archive, which had been counting the alerts one interval at a time, made it 184 hours and 23 minutes across 217 alerts, and entered its objection under *Two clocks, one golf course, and no cake*. A difference of twelve hours was a matter of professional honour. It is possible to love a city by counting its alerts.

The last entry to date was *The doors are closing*. The President had held a press conference of twenty-two minutes and nineteen seconds without a single question he had not rehearsed an answer to, and a BBC correspondent had asked, at the request of colleagues who said that no Ukrainian journalist had managed it, who Vova was on the Mindich tapes. The answer was that she might ask the anti-corruption institutions. The archive went back to its chronology page, where the fatalities and the seconds under alert were being reconciled one by one.

It had been a very confusing year. Two clocks were running in Kyiv, and only one of them was keeping time.

Day 1738. Tomorrow the count would reach 1,739, the number the archive had named when it was thirty days old and 1,709 days away. There was still no way of knowing what the days after it would contain.
