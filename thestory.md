# A record of days

There are several ways to keep a record of the world. One can begin with presidents, armies, treaties and the solemn announcements of men who have mistaken history for their own correspondence. Or one can begin with a morning, a cup of coffee, an overheard conversation, an unexpected noise in the street. The second method is less imposing, but it has one considerable advantage: the world is obliged to make an appearance.

This archive began before the great rupture. Its earlier entries belong to a world that was already showing signs of illness, although, as is often the case with illness, the patient had other things to do. There were politicians, journalists, oligarchs, assorted visionaries and professional custodians of the obvious. There was the familiar theatre of Ukrainian politics, the peculiar gravity of Moscow, the Western talent for misunderstanding events while explaining them at length. There were jokes, irritations, excursions, arguments and the small private business of being alive.

Then came February 24, 2022.

Day 1.

A date can be a calendar mark, but it can also become a border. On one side is the life we thought we were leading. On the other is the life that has been assigned to us. The distinction is not always immediately apparent. People still have suitcases to pack, errands to run, messages to answer. The sun rises with its customary indifference. Somewhere, somebody is looking for a charger. Somewhere else, somebody is trying to decide whether to leave Kyiv.

The war did not abolish ordinary life. It entered it, took up residence and began rearranging the furniture.

From that day onward, the entries became a record of a country at war and of one observer’s attempts to make sense of what was happening around him. Not a military history, not an official chronicle, not a claim to comprehensiveness. The archive has no pretence of being a complete account of Ukraine, and certainly no ambition to speak for everyone who lives here. It is a situated record, written from Kyiv, with all the advantages and limitations of proximity. Sometimes the view is broad. Sometimes it extends only as far as the next street, the nearest metro station or the window from which an explosion can be heard.

There are days when events seem to explain themselves. More often they require explanation, and sometimes they resist it. The absurdity of public life continues even under bombardment. Indeed, it can become more conspicuous. The solemn and the ridiculous travel together, occasionally in the same vehicle. A joke may be an evasion, but it may also be a form of accuracy. We have tried to preserve both the gravity of events and the comedy that refuses to leave them alone.

What follows is therefore not simply a collection of posts arranged by date. It is a record of attention: to people, places, language, power, destruction, resilience and the strange persistence of daily life. The entries accumulate. Their meaning changes as the days pass. A remark made in one year may acquire an unexpected weight in another.

Day 1 is the turning point. It is not the beginning of the story. It is the day the story changed its terms.

## Prelude to war

In the weeks before February 24, 2022, the future was behaving rather badly. It kept sending messages, making telephone calls and appearing in public places, but no one seemed quite certain what to make of it. Some said an invasion was imminent. Others insisted it was not inevitable. Still others explained that the difference between these two words was important, although the distinction seemed to escape a number of people whose professional business was explaining things.

The Russians, meanwhile, were assembling an army on Ukraine’s borders. They were also assembling arguments. Moscow’s preferred version of reality was a curious construction in which Russia had not occupied Crimea, had not started the war in Donbas, had no intention of invading Ukraine and was, in fact, the injured party. It was a remarkable achievement of political architecture. One could almost admire the workmanship, were it not for the rather large number of tanks parked outside.

In Kyiv, the atmosphere was more complicated. American officials were warning of an approaching catastrophe, while Ukrainian officials frequently appeared more concerned about the effects of the warnings than the possibility of the catastrophe itself. There were arguments about blood supplies, intelligence, evacuation plans, airspace, insurance and the proper use of English adverbs. Zelenskyy was reassuring foreign journalists. His advisers were congratulating him on his messaging. The defence minister was telling the country that the likelihood of invasion was low. Somewhere between Washington’s alarms and Kyiv’s reassurances, ordinary people were expected to make up their own minds.

The Western response offered its own spectacle. European leaders travelled to Moscow to visit the Dragon, as though a sufficiently elaborate display of diplomatic courtesy might persuade him to become a house pet. Macron went. Scholz was expected. Everyone had a proposal, a formula or a meeting in principle. The Americans, meanwhile, were warning that the Dragon was not merely hungry but had brought a rather large appetite. Ukraine found itself caught between those who wanted to negotiate away the danger and those who seemed prepared to describe it in such alarming terms that the country itself became part of the damage.

There was also the familiar procession of experts, commentators, journalists and professional Ukraine enthusiasts. Some were useful. Others had discovered that proximity to a crisis was an excellent way to acquire an audience, even if one had little to say. The distinction between reporting and performance became increasingly difficult to maintain. We watched people manufacture certainty from fragments of intelligence, repeat rumours as analysis and congratulate themselves for having understood a country they had barely bothered to observe.

And yet, beneath the noise, something serious was taking shape. The Kremlin’s demands were becoming less obscure. Its language was becoming more openly imperial. The possibility that Russia might attempt to destroy Ukrainian independence was no longer a theoretical exercise conducted by people with maps and television studios. The question was how far Moscow intended to go, and how much of the world was prepared to let it.

These entries were written without knowledge of what would happen next. They contain impatience, sarcasm, arguments, occasional excursions into chicken recipes and the usual irritation with people who mistake confidence for competence. They also contain a growing recognition that the situation was becoming dangerous in ways that no diplomatic euphemism could conceal.

On February 23, the story was still being described as a tragicomedy. The next morning, the comedy would become considerably harder to sustain.

Day 1 was approaching. The prelude was almost over.

## The first thirty days

On February 24, 2022, the argument ended. Russia invaded Ukraine, and the future that had been discussed, predicted, denied and occasionally mocked arrived in the form of missiles, explosions and columns of military vehicles.

The first entries are full of movement and uncertainty. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Wandering around with suitcases</strong></span> (Day 1). <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Remember Det-A?</strong></span> (Day 2), an in-joke about a reunion of the clandestine US Army Special Forces unit once based in West Berlin. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Enemy sabotage groups</strong></span> (Day 2ish). Reports came from Hostomel, Obolon, Vasylkiv and the streets of Kyiv. There were sirens, gunfire, roadblocks and rumours of infiltrators. Residential buildings were struck. Bridges were blown up. The capital was preparing to defend itself.

The city nevertheless continued to function, after a fashion. Curfew ended. People went to work. A mother pushed a baby carriage loaded with water containers through an almost empty centre. The weather was cold and bright. There were children in camouflage and civilians carrying weapons. The ordinary world had not disappeared. It had acquired an unfamiliar set of instructions.

The first week brought its own vocabulary: <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Demonstrative acts of extreme violence</strong></span> (Day 6). <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Giant pink blob</strong></span> (Day 7), the pink mass on the Institute for the Study of War’s map showing Russian forces moving down from Belarus toward Kyiv. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Stay out of doorways!</strong></span> (Day 8) The enormous Russian convoy approaching Kyiv became the principal object of military speculation. Maps multiplied, experts debated and television correspondents explained. The convoy stalled. The invasion’s promised lightning victory was becoming something else.

The early confidence of the Russian leadership rested on a remarkable misunderstanding of Ukraine. The country was expected to submit, its government to disappear and its population to welcome a new administration. Even reports from inside the Russian security apparatus suggested that the plan had come apart. Denazification and demilitarisation were proving difficult to translate into measurable achievements.

Meanwhile, the geography of the war became painfully familiar. Borodyanka, Irpin and Bucha were no longer names on maps. Russian troops moved through towns and villages around Kyiv, occupying buildings, terrorising residents and killing civilians. People attempting to escape were shot. The destruction of Borodyanka was particularly difficult to comprehend. A place that had once been a familiar destination for a bicycle ride had become a landscape of ruins.

Farther away, Mariupol was being subjected to a siege of extraordinary cruelty. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Mariupol massacre</strong></span> (Day 15). The bombing of a maternity hospital. Hundreds of thousands of people trapped, without reliable access to food, water or medical care. The war was no longer merely an attempt to capture territory. It was an assault on the people who lived there.

And still, the archive retained its peculiar sense of proportion. A shoe was repaired because the repairmen were busy fighting the invaders. A turtle named Torpedo was rescued after a missile strike in Obolon. A cruise missile was intercepted over Kyiv. The wreckage fell in Kurenivka. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Torpedo the flying turtle</strong></span> (Day 20) became, briefly, an entry in the history of military affairs.

The absurdities did not make the violence less serious. They made the world recognisable.

By the third week, the maps showed Russian forces digging in around Kyiv rather than advancing towards it. The approaches to the capital, the roads and settlements west, north and east of the city, became increasingly important. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>More smart optics, please</strong></span> (Day 24). The war had acquired a geography of its own, and understanding it required more than coloured shapes and confident television commentary.

The international conversation was becoming familiar, too. Sanctions, negotiations, red lines and assurances that Western governments were not parties to the conflict. Ukraine was receiving weapons and intelligence, but it needed more. Advice was plentiful. Ammunition was more useful.

The archive was also recording the curious population that gathers around every catastrophe: serious volunteers, military specialists, journalists, commentators, adventurers and people who seem to regard danger as a promising career opportunity. Some were doing indispensable work. Others were performing for an audience. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Mad Max</strong></span> (Day 29). <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>TikTok legionnaires</strong></span> (Day 30). Even a war has its travelling circus.

On March 25, the thirtieth day, Kyiv was still standing. The original Russian plan had failed. The government remained in place, the armed forces continued to resist, and the capital had not been captured.

But the month did not end in triumph. Mariupol remained under siege. Towns around Kyiv lay devastated. Millions of people had been displaced. The dead could not be restored by the failure of the invasion’s opening campaign.

Thirty days had altered the meaning of ordinary things. A journey of forty kilometres could become a military operation. A walk to work could be an act of endurance. A repaired shoe, a rescued turtle and a quiet morning could become matters worth recording.

The archive had begun as a collection of observations. It was now something else: a record of a country refusing to disappear.

There were still 1,709 days to go before the count reached 1,739. There was no way of knowing what they would contain.

## The next three months

The first month ended with Kyiv still standing and the Russian army looking for another way to win a war it had expected to finish in a few days. The next three months would be devoted to discovering what that other way might look like.

The geography changed. The enormous convoy that had threatened the capital became a memory. The fighting moved east and south, towards Donbas, where the names of towns began to accumulate with the grim regularity of entries in a ledger. The first phase was over, though the archive would not formally announce it until April 21, under the rather belated heading <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Phase 1 ended on March 25</strong></span> (Day 57).

There was a brief temptation to believe that the worst had passed. Kyiv began to breathe again. People returned. Cars appeared on familiar roads. The city grew greener, cafés prepared to reopen, and the monuments received the attention usually reserved for visiting dignitaries. But the places left behind by the Russian army were telling a different story. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>One big crime scene</strong></span> (Day 40) was not a metaphor. Bucha, Irpin and Borodyanka had become evidence. The dead were no longer distant figures in daily reports. Their bodies, and the houses around them, recorded what the occupation had meant.

The war continued, and the arguments about it multiplied. There were maps, phases, forecasts, weapons lists and elaborate theories about what Putin might do next. There were also the familiar experts, some of whom had been wrong before the invasion and were now being invited to explain its progress. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Outrageous claims to expertise</strong></span> (Day 53) became a recurring subject. The archive was learning that a war could be fought on several fronts at once, and that not all of them were military.

In May, the Russian offensive in the east became the central concern. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The Popasna salient</strong></span> (Day 86). <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The Sievierodonetsk salient</strong></span> (Day 88). <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The Lyman salient</strong></span> (Day 92). The names were less familiar than Kyiv’s suburbs, but the arithmetic was becoming unmistakable. Russia was concentrating its forces, destroying towns and trying to grind its way forward. Ukraine needed weapons, ammunition and equipment. The requests were repeated so often that they began to sound less like diplomacy than a household reminder left on the kitchen table.

There was a curious interval between the announcement of assistance and its arrival. Politicians made statements. Officials discussed deliveries. Analysts assessed capabilities. Somewhere in the middle, soldiers needed the equipment. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Weapons, weapons, weapons</strong></span> (Day 94) was not an especially sophisticated strategic doctrine, but it had the advantage of being comprehensible.

Mariupol remained the great wound. Its siege, the destruction of the city and the fate of the defenders at Azovstal stood behind the daily accounts of fighting elsewhere. The Russian parade on May 9 offered its own grotesque theatre. Moscow celebrated victory over fascism while prosecuting a war of destruction against a neighbouring country. History, as usual, had been invited to provide the decorations.

By June, the first hundred days had passed. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>100-Day wrap-up</strong></span> (Day 100) was a convenient label for an inconvenient reality. The invasion had failed to achieve its original objectives, but the war was nowhere near over. Russian forces continued to advance in parts of Donbas. Ukrainian cities remained under attack. The supply of Western weapons was still a matter of argument, negotiation and delay.

There were lighter entries, too. A vegetable preference involving pelmeni. A farm visited by Mario, Emmanuel and Olaf. The occasional encounter with a visitor, a journalist or an expert whose understanding of Ukraine seemed inversely proportional to the confidence with which it was expressed. Such things belonged in the record. They were not distractions from the war so much as evidence that life had not agreed to become exclusively about it.

The chapter closes in late June with a simple instruction: <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Eat, sleep, fight</strong></span> (Day 121). By then the war had acquired a rhythm, though not a predictable one. The first shock had given way to endurance. The capital was no longer in immediate danger of occupation, but the country was paying for that reprieve in the east.

Three months had passed. The invasion had not ended. It had changed shape, and so had the archive.

## The long middle

By the summer of 2022, the war had settled into a new and less comprehensible shape. Kyiv was no longer the immediate prize. The Russian army had been driven from the capital’s approaches, but the country remained under attack along a front stretching from the northeast to the Black Sea. The first shock had passed. The war was becoming a test of endurance, equipment, organisation and patience. Patience, as it turned out, was in particularly short supply.

The summer brought a great deal of waiting. Weapons were arriving, but never quite quickly enough. The front moved slowly. Russian forces dug in, Ukrainian forces prepared, and the daily maps offered more coloured shapes than useful answers. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>More stalemate and energetic dancing</strong></span> (Day 174) was the diagnosis in August. The prospect of a counteroffensive in Kherson was discussed with growing anticipation, though the people doing the actual fighting were rather less inclined to advertise their plans.

Behind the front lines, occupation had its own geography. Kherson was not simply a city waiting to be liberated. It was a region of abductions, intimidation, filtration and disappearance. The accounts were difficult to reconcile with the tidy language of military analysis. A map could show who controlled a road. It could not show what happened in a house after dark.

Then came September, and the pivot. Ukrainian forces broke through Russian positions in the Kharkiv region, recovering territory with a speed that unsettled the usual commentators. The maps changed almost overnight. Russian troops abandoned equipment and positions, and the confident language of Moscow’s military announcements became increasingly difficult to maintain. Kherson remained a harder problem, but the direction of travel was becoming clearer.

Putin responded with mobilisation and the annexation of territory his army did not fully control. The ceremonies in Moscow were conducted with the customary solemnity, as though a document and a stage set could accomplish what the military had failed to do. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Putin’s doom loop</strong></span> (Day 219) was an apt title. The invasion had produced a sequence of decisions that narrowed his options while making the consequences more dangerous.

October brought the war back to the cities with renewed force. On October 10, Russian missiles struck targets across Ukraine, including Kyiv. The attacks on civilian and energy infrastructure became a campaign of their own. Bridges, power stations, heating systems and the ordinary machinery of urban life were now part of the battlefield. The capital’s glass bridge narrowly escaped destruction. The crater was a reminder that the distance between a morning run and a missile strike could be measured in metres.

The rhetoric in Moscow grew stranger as the military situation deteriorated. Nazism gave way to Satanism. Dirty bombs and combat mosquitoes joined the catalogue of inventions. The explanations changed, but the purpose remained the same: to make the destruction of Ukraine sound like an act of defence.

By November, the war had entered the apartment. Electricity disappeared for hours. Water became uncertain. Elevators stopped, heating systems faltered and the city learned to organise itself around the availability of a socket. The government established points of invincibility, which was an unusually ambitious name for a place where one might charge a telephone and warm one’s hands. Kherson was liberated, but the relief came with the knowledge of what its residents had endured and what the retreating Russians had left behind.

December brought mud, cold, generators and another round of missile attacks. The war was approaching its three-hundredth day. The capital was still functioning, though not always according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The archive kept returning to the same questions: what could be destroyed, what could be defended, and what kind of country would emerge from the wreckage?

The year ended with <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The empty chair technique</strong></span> (Day 308) and a presidential performance that seemed to mistake defiance for infallibility. There were still arguments about weapons, strategy, corruption, foreign assistance and the people appointed to explain it all. The war had not made Ukrainian public life simpler. It had made its contradictions more expensive.

At the start of 2023, Russia had failed to conquer Ukraine. Ukraine had recovered substantial territory, but much remained occupied. The front was still there. So were the missiles, the dead, the displaced and the long winter ahead.

The first thirty days had been a shock. The next three months had established the war’s geography. The remainder of 2022 showed what it would cost to change it.

## The year of the long argument

The year opened with <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Splodges of tomato paste</strong></span> (Day 313), a small meditation on Twitter, its expanding character limit and the possibility that giving people more room might not make them think any better. It was an appropriate beginning. The war was entering its second year, and there was no shortage of people with room to explain it. The January entry <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Pre-experiencing Ukraine's PTSD</strong></span> (Day 316) turned its attention to the material being piped through the internet, much of it garbage, with the occasional unopened bottle of beer to be found in the dumpster.

The practical questions were less entertaining. Ukraine needed ammunition, night vision, air defence and long-range weapons. Russia had a sanctuary in occupied Crimea from which to attack. NATO officials spoke of strategy while resisting the implications of their own analysis. The archive's January title <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>NATO wants us dead</strong></span> (Day 332) was not a diplomatic formulation. Nor was it intended to be. On February 24, <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Content-free peace</strong></span> (Day 366) marked the anniversary. The sun was bright over Kyiv. China had produced a twelve-point plan, a word salad that did not require much digestion. The war had entered its second year, but the argument about what would end it had barely moved.

There were questions closer to home. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The sham democracy</strong></span> (Day 365) appeared on the eve of the anniversary, reminding readers that the invasion had not abolished Ukraine's political problems. Corruption, appointments, the conduct of Z’s office and the weakness of institutions remained matters of consequence. They did not become less important because Russia was trying to destroy the country. The archive had little interest in the idea that wartime criticism was somehow a luxury.

Spring brought the counteroffensive into public conversation long before it could be brought into operation. The expectations grew faster than the supply of equipment. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Counteroffensive bloat</strong></span> (Day 438) was the archive's diagnosis of the atmosphere, as journalists and experts competed to predict an operation whose details Ukraine had good reason not to advertise. In April the old Dragon returned. In Evgeny Schwartz's parable, the monster was not destroying the enemy because it had not yet had its fill of war. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The dragons are back</strong></span> (Day 429) and <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Dragon, the parable</strong></span> (Day 434) were more than literary diversions. The archive saw something familiar in the spectacle of a country waiting for its own deliverance while the people with the power to provide it continued to discuss the terms.

In June, Russia blew up the Kakhovka dam. The flood spread through the lower Dnipro, carrying away homes, roads, animals and the comfortable fiction that civilian infrastructure was somehow outside the war. The archive called it <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>After the Kakhovka dam</strong></span> (Day 468ish). A few weeks later, Yevgeny Prigozhin sent his Wagner column towards Moscow. The mutiny briefly exposed the peculiar foundations of Russian power. In Kyiv, meanwhile, cobblestones on Bohdan Khmelnytsky Street were being lifted, inspected and put back at considerable public expense. The war and the paving work occupied the same city. They were not competing for space.

July brought the death of Victoria Amelina, who had been documenting Russian war crimes. The archive's entry <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The death of Victoria Amelina</strong></span> (Day 495) appeared amid the continuing arguments about the counteroffensive. At Vilnius, NATO offered Ukraine a future invitation without a timetable. Two months into the operation, <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The collective minefield</strong></span> (Day 508) described the practical obstacle: Ukrainian forces still lacked the equipment to negotiate the minefields Russian troops had spent months laying. There was not much mystery about what was needed. There was a great deal of discussion about whether it would be supplied.

Prigozhin was dead by August. The counteroffensive continued, though the maps became less encouraging. The front moved very little. In November, <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The battlefield stalemate</strong></span> (Day 623) gave the situation its plainest name. Zaluzhny had explained what would be required to move from positional to manoeuvre warfare. The argument was not especially complicated. The means of doing it were another matter.

Meanwhile, the war in Gaza competed for attention, American aid became entangled in domestic politics, and another winter approached. The archive marked <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Ten years of war</strong></span> (Day 631), a reminder that February 2022 had not created the conflict. It had enlarged it beyond the scale at which the outside world could comfortably pretend not to notice.

December brought a new round of arguments about mobilisation, Z and the Commander-in-Chief. Aid was in doubt, the front remained largely fixed, and the largest air attack of the war struck Ukraine on December 29.

The year ended without a settlement, a breakthrough or a convincing explanation from the people paid to supply them. The archive had begun by proposing to lift the curtain on the nonsense surrounding the war. It had found that the curtain was part of the scenery.

The arguments continued. The war did not wait for them to finish.

Day 674. The next year was already arriving.

## The year of the thinning line

The year began with a digression to Taipei. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The road to Taipei City</strong></span> (Day 677) compared two electoral democracies of roughly equal size, each threatened by a larger neighbour. It was an unusual way to begin a year in which Ukraine would once again depend on decisions made far away. The last week of December had brought the largest air attack of the war. Congress had gone home, the Speaker was busy with his telephone, and Trump was recovering on a tropical island.

The shortages were becoming harder to disguise. Ukraine needed shells, air defence and men. The third shortage was the most difficult to discuss. The mobilisation argument that had simmered since the autumn came to the boil, while Z and the Commander-in-Chief contradicted each other with increasing regularity. The weeks-long dismissal of Valeriy Zaluzhny was conducted as a tragicomedy, announced, denied, postponed and finally delivered on February 8. Nine days later Avdiivka was abandoned. The foreign press called it the Kremlin's first great victory since Bakhmut. The archive was less impressed by the terminology. The purpose of the battle had been to kill as many invaders as possible, and the invaders had been killed in considerable numbers. Two days later came <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Requiem</strong></span> (Day 726). There was no attempt to make the loss sound better than it was.

The spring belonged to Washington. For months, Speaker Mike Johnson had conducted his private foreign policy through telephone calls, White House visits and the strategic deployment of evangelical hesitation. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Mike Johnson treads carefully</strong></span> (Day 777) was one of the archive's more economical descriptions of the performance. In April the aid bill finally passed. Relief was real, but the delay had already acquired consequences. Russian forces broke through at Ocheretyne, and in May a new offensive began near Kharkiv. The invaders advanced against Ukrainian troops who were outnumbered and exhausted. The archive had been warning about the missing ammunition for months. The maps now supplied the illustration.

There were other reasons to be anxious. In March, gunmen attacked a concert hall near Moscow, and the Kremlin hurried to assemble an explanation in which Ukraine had a part. A Tsirkon missile came down over Kyiv. By June, electricity was again being rationed, and the city had relearned the habits of the previous winter, this time with summer heat added to the inconvenience. Day 800 passed in May. In June, the war entered its eleventh year. Neither was a celebration. The count simply continued.

Then, in August, Ukrainian forces crossed into Kursk region. The operation surprised Moscow and, by most accounts, quite a few people in Kyiv. It lifted morale and gave the commentators something new to explain. It did not, however, stop the Russian advance in Donetsk region. The archive put the two developments beside each other under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Pokrovsk packs its bags</strong></span> (Day 922). A bold stroke in one place had not changed the arithmetic in another. Toretsk was in trouble, Vuhledar fell, and Kurakhove was next in line. North Korea supplied ballistic missiles, and by December its soldiers had joined the fighting in Kursk region.

In September Z carried his Victory Plan to Washington and discovered that it had become a minor episode in an American election. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The Victory Plan backfires</strong></span> (Day 946) was the archive's verdict. In November Donald Trump won. The archive called it <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Mandate for mayhem</strong></span> (Day 987). A quick end to the war was now being promised by a man who would have to persuade the other side to agree. The Economist suggested that the fighting might wind down in 2025. Somebody proposed that Russia keep Crimea. The archive answered with <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Peace talks, nuclear annihilation</strong></span> (Day 1002). It was not in a mood to negotiate away other people's territory.

The winter brought a new missile, Oreshnik, and the old problem of trying to understand what Moscow wanted the world to believe about it. Western governments had finally allowed Ukraine to use long-range missiles against targets in Russia. Putin announced that this meant direct Western participation in the war. The missile reportedly carried no explosives. Its owner was <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The maniac next door</strong></span> (Day 1010). At home, defective mortar shells continued to reach front-line units, with no one taking responsibility for the failure. In December a Russian general in charge of chemical and biological defence was killed by a <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Scooter bomb</strong></span> (Day 1028). Moscow's air defence apparently shot down an Azerbaijani passenger plane.

Through it all there were tulips in April, and <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The cherry blossoms survive</strong></span> (Day 787). There was stair-running in the dark during the blackouts, a giant goldfish in Iceland and a bicycle brought out of winter storage. Such things were not interruptions. They were the other half of the record.

By December the thousandth day and the beginning of a third winter belonged to the same unfinished sentence. The line had thinned, though it had not broken. Ukraine had lost ground, and learned again how much of its future depended on decisions made elsewhere.

On December 31, ballistic and cruise missiles were over Kyiv. At least one MiG-31 remained airborne when the archive made its last entry of the year, under the title <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Quels cons</strong></span> (Day 1042).

Day 1042. The next year was already arriving.

## The year of the peace process

The year began with a Russian drone striking a residential flat near the President’s Office in Kyiv. Two people were killed across the city. Hours later, Z delivered his New Year’s address with the Motherland Monument over his shoulder. It also noticed the 155th Mechanized Brigade. France had trained and equipped it. Before it fired its first shot, 1,700 soldiers had gone absent without leave. Its commander was dismissed as it entered battle. A senior officer responsible for its formation died of a heart attack. On January 7, under the heading <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Four systemic failures</strong></span> (Day 1049), the archive listed the problems again: fortifications, conscription, new brigades and domestic arms production. A week later it put a number on the manpower crisis: one soldier in ten absent without leave.

There was no shortage of plans. The Peace Formula, the Victory Plan and the Resilience Plan had all been announced, discussed or forgotten. The question was whether the country could organise itself for a war already in its fourth year. The archive quoted a brigade commander who said that only domestic policy could mobilise society, and that without justice the struggle itself was impossible. It was one of the year’s less amusing observations.

The new American administration arrived promising to end the war quickly. Kyiv responded with flattery, as did much of Europe. The spectacle belonged under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Golden age of brown noses</strong></span> (Day 1078). On February 28, the cameras caught the argument in the Oval Office. The minerals agreement remained unsigned. Within days Washington suspended military assistance. The rupture was public, but the problems it exposed were not new. On March 1, an Iskander missile struck a training camp in the Dnipropetrovsk region, killing and wounding more than a hundred recruits from the hastily assembled 157th Mechanized Brigade. The commander of the land forces promised that those responsible would answer for it.

Then came Sumy. On Palm Sunday, two Russian ballistic missiles struck the city centre, killing more than a dozen people, including six children, and wounding scores. A few hours earlier, Trump had said the talks were going fine. The entry was titled <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Palm Sunday massacre</strong></span> (Day 1145). It added only that the talks were not going fine.

In May, delegations met in Istanbul. A thirty-day truce was proposed and ignored. The process moved at the pace Moscow preferred. On June 1, Ukraine acted without waiting for another meeting. Drones concealed in shipping containers disguised as modular homes were launched against Russian airfields. The archive’s count put the damage at more than a third of Russia’s strategic bomber fleet. It was one of the few consequential events of the year that did not depend on somebody else’s telephone call.

In August, Witkoff went to see Putin. The archive called it <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The Neverland summit</strong></span> (Day 1262), giving the American envoy pixie dust and Putin a hook. A week later Trump and Putin met at a military base in Anchorage. The photographs were taken. The terms followed. Russia wanted Ukraine out of Donbas, non-aligned and stripped of the right to choose its own future. The archive filed them under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Terms of our surrender</strong></span> (Day 1271). Kyiv and the Europeans offered a counter-proposal. Nothing was agreed. At the end of the month, Andriy Parubiy, former speaker of parliament, was shot dead in Lviv. Police said the killing had been carefully prepared.

The autumn brought the war back to the cities. Russian drones crossed into Poland. NATO recalled that Article 5 existed and issued a statement. The archive, remembering Zaluzhny’s warning about the alliance becoming another United Nations, called the episode <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>NATO’s funeral pyre</strong></span> (Day 1296). In October Putin threatened that Tomahawk missiles would destroy relations with Washington, while his forces continued bombing Ukrainian cities. On October 10, a drone struck a seventeen-storey building in Pechersk at around two in the morning. Half of Kyiv lost power. Two days earlier the archive had written <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Turning off the lights</strong></span> (Day 1323). It recorded the night under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Kyiv loses power</strong></span> (Day 1325). The front was moving too. An officer publicly asked for an order to withdraw from Pokrovsk and Myrnohrad before the garrisons were lost.

In the third week of November, two stories arrived together. Washington produced a twenty-eight-point peace plan that the archive recognised as Putin’s maximalist demands from Istanbul, now carrying an American cover. At the same time, anti-corruption investigators disclosed Operation Midas: more than a thousand wiretaps collected over fifteen months, documenting a kickback scheme at the heart of the energy sector. The archive joined the two under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Surrender diplomacy meets golden toilet mafia</strong></span> (Day 1367). Andriy Yermak, Z’s chief of staff, resigned on November 28. Witkoff and Jared Kushner went to Moscow. A telephone call Witkoff apparently placed to someone in Moscow by accident became <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Witkoff’s Moscow butt dial</strong></span> (Day 1372). By December, the archive was reporting on <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The peace plan nobody read</strong></span> (Day 1386).

A twenty-point version followed. Europe agreed to borrow ninety billion euros to keep Ukraine solvent for two more years, while the idea of using frozen Russian assets was set aside. In Kyiv, the lights were going out. DTEK’s outage schedule divided the evenings into blocks of darkness. The diplomats logged thirty hours of meetings in a week. Z said he was tired of meetings for the sake of meetings.

The archive kept other appointments. There were full moons, Christmas decorations that remained up until the end of April, spring in Kyiv, and the return of dead hangs and deep squats. These were not interruptions. They were the ordinary measures by which a city continued to exist.

The last entry was about resilience centres in Vyshhorod, north of Kyiv, where people sat through days-long power cuts in crowded shelters. The archive paired the report with a photograph by Luc Delahaye from a 2006 rally in Minsk for Belarusian opposition leader Alaksandar Milinkievič. One picture showed people trying to keep warm in a town under attack. The other showed people trying to keep a country open. The years and the border between them did not make the connection less clear.

The diplomats had spent the year drawing up plans for a future. In Vyshhorod, people were trying to get through the present.

The peace process had produced a great many plans and no peace. The war had become the one reliable fixture in a year of promises.

Day 1406. The next year was already arriving.


## The year of two clocks

The year opened with a question: was it a year to forget, or regret? On December 31, the *New York Times* had published eighteen and a half thousand words on how Washington had helped Moscow prolong the war. Trump answered with a pep talk. The archive went out into the cold.

There were two clocks in Kyiv. One belonged to the diplomats, who kept announcing that something was about to happen. The other belonged to the city, which measured the year in outages, air raids and the hours until the heating came back.

The cold was the first great subject. <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Welcome to the blockade</strong></span> (Day 1416 contd), the archive wrote in January. A city that had once held three and a half million people now held perhaps three million. In one night Russia launched 242 drones and thirteen ballistic missiles, with an Oreshnik thrown in for Lviv. Electricity went off in the morning and returned in the evening. At minus 25 degrees, more than 1,100 high-rise buildings in two districts were without heat. The archive traced the cold to Moscow’s attacks and to district offices, where insulation work in 119 buildings had been contracted at inflated prices and a fifth of the money had gone elsewhere. The government celebrated <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Nine Megawatts of Victory</strong></span> (Day 1447), the extra power allotted to the capital, at a wholesale cost of 765 euros an hour. Nobody was ungrateful. It was only that gratitude had begun to require arithmetic.

The archive’s contribution to the peace process was a Valentine’s Day story about Napoleon cakes, a fitness-studio regular whom rumour had promoted to sniper, and a capitulation with custard on top. It was called <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The art of the non-deal</strong></span> (Day 1455). Diplomacy was having a very good winter. Trump announced that the talks were going well, then that they had been very, very good and that something could be happening. The fourth anniversary of the invasion passed on February 24. The archive wrote about hollow columns, which look sound until asked to bear a load. Two days later, in Geneva, two men discussed Iran’s nuclear programme in the morning and Ukraine’s survival after lunch, with the same smiles for the same photographers. The archive called it <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The golden age of hegemony</strong></span> (Day 1468). In March Washington’s attention moved to Iran, and Ukraine became the second item on the agenda.

By April, a proposal had emerged to name a depopulated strip of coal country in the Dnipropetrovsk region after the most powerful man in the world. It was called <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Donnyland</strong></span> (Day 1518). The people who lived there, apparently, were expected to kneel. In May, anti-corruption investigators released a recording, allegedly of a conversation between Z’s former chief of staff and his house designer, dated February 16, 2022, eight days before the invasion. The archive called the entry <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The house burns</strong></span> (Day 1540). In June it published a preamble to itself, <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The map keeps getting larger</strong></span> (Day 1582).

July was not quiet. A Russian strike on Kyiv came in its usual order: ballistic missiles, jet-propelled drones, cruise missiles and more drones. The city carried foam mats to the metro. An apartment block in Podil, a hotel, an ambulance station and a monastery with a UNESCO plaque were among the places hit. A few days later, Vyshneve, twelve kilometres from the capital, was devastated. Houses burned to their foundations. Six hundred people were evacuated, and fifty more had to be taken out by armoured transport because the roads themselves had become targets. Z reduced the destruction to a clause about secondary detonation. The phrase <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Durable until Tuesday</strong></span> (Day 1594) was the archive’s verdict. So it goes.

Politics produced its own weather. Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov was dismissed and gave an exit briefing that named everyone except the person who mattered. Outside, on the square beside the Ivan Franko Theatre, people gathered, but their signs were aimed at the commander-in-chief, Syrsky, who had kept his job. A year earlier Z had signed the law that gutted the independence of the anti-corruption agencies and provoked the largest protests of the war. By August the government had been dismissed, leaving the country without a defence minister or a foreign minister. At Myla, twenty-five kilometres from Kyiv, an arms depot exploded. At least thirty-eight people were killed.

By September, the disorder had moved indoors. A shootout between HUR, Ukraine’s military intelligence service, and the SBU, its domestic security service, centred on Stepan Kaplunov, a deputy commander of the Russian Volunteer Corps, a formation of Russians fighting on Ukraine’s side. HUR had come to rescue him from men it believed were holding him. The men turned out to be SBU officers. It was less a spy thriller than a turf war between armed men who had forgotten where the fence stood.

On September 25, a Russian drone struck the tenth and eleventh floors of a Pechersk apartment block where I had lived for four years. I now lived two hundred metres away. The school beside the block was thirty metres from it. A fourteen-year-old boy was killed and at least seven residents were injured. The archive wrote: <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>There are days when geography becomes literature all by itself.</strong></span> (Day 1678) That same week, another attack on Kyiv’s network infrastructure revealed a new tactic: two jet-powered drones working in tandem, one carrying the weapon, the other relaying signals and filming. On September 28, a drone struck the National Academy of Sciences and a hospital. The Academy’s building partly collapsed and caught fire. At least two people were killed.

The diplomatic traffic had no front line, only a succession of hotels and airports. Envoys shuttled between Paris, Abu Dhabi, Geneva and Miami. The Ukrainian saying for the result is that they married him off without him. The archive wrote <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Washington hands Moscow the table</strong></span> (Day 1691 contd). In October the First Lady’s office announced a local ceasefire at Oleshky, where residents were foraging for weeds, so civilians might leave. The archive filed it under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Oleshky for Election Day</strong></span> (Day 1706). On November 5 Z promised a <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Big, beautiful agreement</strong></span> (Day 1716), which would involve more than Ukraine, perhaps all of Europe, and in which everyone would be thrilled. The same week, Kyiv learned that senior law-enforcement officials had been working with, or for, a drug cartel with Russian connections based in Mexico.

In Berlin, a spokesman announced that four principles had been agreed in Abu Dhabi. Governments rarely announce their principles, the archive remarked. They prefer to let them be discovered later, like an old coat in a wardrobe. On November 19, the leaders of America, Russia and China met at Xi’s invitation and issued a declaration about reforming the United Nations, avoiding nuclear war and limiting the reach of international courts. The archive filed it under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Declared impunity</strong></span> (Day 1730). The photograph that accompanied it had been taken in the Kyiv metro during a combined air assault.

A Washington columnist reported that Kyiv had spent 172 hours under air alert in October. The archive, which had counted each interval, made it 184 hours and 23 minutes across 217 alerts. It entered its objection under <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>Two clocks, one golf course, and no cake</strong></span> (Day 1734). A difference of twelve hours was a matter of professional honour. It is possible to love a city by counting its alerts.

Elsewhere, people waited for electricity, fitted errands between alerts and found ways to keep ordinary routines alive. These were not interruptions from the war. They were the narrow spaces the war left for living.

The latest entry was <span style="color: #c62828"><strong>The doors are closing</strong></span> (Day 1738). Z held a press conference lasting twenty-two minutes and nineteen seconds. A BBC correspondent asked who “Vova” referred to in the Mindich recordings, part of an inquiry into alleged corruption at the heart of the energy sector. Z replied that she might ask the anti-corruption institutions.

It had been a very confusing year. Two clocks were running in Kyiv, and only one of them was keeping time.

Day 1738. Tomorrow the count would reach 1,739. The number had once been a distant marker, noted almost in passing. Now it was simply tomorrow. The lights might go out again. The alerts might sound. The next entry had not yet been written.
